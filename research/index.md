My research seeks to understand how marine species respond and adapt to rapid environmental change, from short-lived zooplankton to long-lived marine mammals and fishes. I use diverse approaches including physiology, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and population genomics to investigate the mechanistic and evolutionary underpinnings of ecologically relevant traits, with the goal of helping sustain resilient populations and ecosystems while furthering our understanding of evolutionary processes.

I am particularly interested in leveraging natural environmental gradients, such as salinity and temperature, along with experimental evolution in the lab to understand adaptive processes.

## Genomics for marine mammal and fisheries management

At NOAA's [Southeast Fisheries Science Center](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/about/marine-mammal-and-turtle-division-southeast-fisheries-science-center), I apply genomics to the management of marine mammals, turtles, and fisheries species. This work applies the approaches and lessons learned from short-lived, experimentally tractable species such as copepods to longer-lived species of direct conservation and management concern.

- **Rice's whale:** In this critically endangered whale, with only ~60 individuals remaining, I am using genomic and epigenomic data from nearly 70% of the population to estimate physiological health, inbreeding, and family structure.
- **Bottlenose dolphins:** I identified a bottlenose dolphin hybrid zone and developed environmental predictors to discriminate stocks (Brennan et al., in review).
- **Sperm whales:** Sex-specific dispersal maintains mitochondrial structure despite nuclear panmixia in Gulf of Mexico sperm whales ([Brennan et al., 2026, *Heredity*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41437-026-00824-x)).

### Epigenetic clocks for age estimation

Age is fundamental to managing wild populations: it underlies estimates of growth, maturity, mortality, and population trajectories. Yet for many marine species, age is difficult or impossible to measure directly, particularly from the small, non-lethal tissue samples typically available for long-lived and protected species. Epigenetic clocks use age-associated changes in DNA methylation to estimate age from these samples.

I am leading the development of clocks for a number of marine fishes.

## Susceptibility and resilience to global change

As the environment rapidly changes due to human causes, it is essential to understand how populations may respond. These responses can consist of adaptation, plasticity, or a combination of the two (in addition to migration or extinction). We use a variety of approaches to predict the relative impact of these different mechanisms to determine if and how ecosystems may be resilient to global change.

### Experimental evolution 

One method to determine if evolutionary rescue can help populations respond to changing climates is experimental evolution. Using marine copepods, we conduct  multi-generational lab based experimental evolution studies under multiple stressor conditions. In this work we are particularly interested in understanding:

1. Do species have the capacity to evolve to predicted, multi-stressor future conditions?
2. How do adaptation and plasticity interact and affect one another during evolutionary rescue?
3. Are there costs associated with rapid adaptation?

We have found that adaptation to simultaneous warming and acidification is rapid but limited ([Dam et al., 2021, *Nature Climate Change*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-021-01131-5)), that adaptation to multiple stressors is polygenic and synergistic ([Brennan et al., 2022, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2201521119)), that genetic and epigenetic changes act together to facilitate rapid adaptation ([Brennan et al., 2025, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422782122)), and that evolutionary rescue comes at the cost of reduced plasticity ([Brennan et al., 2022, *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-28742-6)).

### Predictions of local adaptation and susceptibility to global change

To effectively manage species, it is fundamental to be able to predict the susceptibility of populations to future conditions. One increasingly used approach is genomic vulnerability, which predicts susceptibility from patterns of genetic variation in the wild, but its link to physiological tolerance remains largely untested. With funding from the [DFG](https://www.dfg.de/en/index.jsp), former PhD student [Alexandra Hahn](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=d_Foh7wAAAAJ&hl=en) and I tested these predictions in the copepod *Acartia tonsa* across the Baltic Sea. We found that this species is locally adapted to salinity ([Hahn et al., 2026, *Molecular Ecology*](https://doi.org/10.1111/mec.70208)), but that genomic vulnerability poorly predicts physiological performance. Instead, we developed a physiological predictor of habitat suitability and resilience based on fitness measured across populations ([Hahn et al., bioRxiv](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.11.13.766083)).

## Temporal genomics and fluctuating selection

Populations regularly experience huge environmental fluctuations across time, both within and across years. These seasonal fluctuations likely contribute to the maintenance of adaptive genetic variation. We might also predict that this same adaptive genetic variation would contribute to global change adaptation, particularly for stressors such as temperature.

Species with short generation times provide a natural experiment for testing how populations respond to environmental change. In the copepod *Acartia hudsonica*, work led by [Alexandra Hahn](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=d_Foh7wAAAAJ&hl=en) showed that seasonal shifts in thermal tolerance are driven by plasticity rather than adaptation ([Hahn and Brennan, 2024, *J. Exp. Mar. Biol. Ecol.*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jembe.2024.152014)). 

We are also leveraging biweekly collections of the copepod *Eurytemora affinis* sampled since 2009 to identify the genomic signal of fluctuating selection. This work led by former postdoc, [Jenny Nascimento-Schulze](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=mtE7iscAAAAJ&hl=en), has found consistent signals of fluctuating selection across years. We are pairing these results with experiments to reveal the physiological, epigenomic, and transcriptomic consequences of fluctuating selection.

I have also applied temporal genomic approaches to long-lived species, including evidence of fisheries-induced evolution in Eastern Baltic cod ([Han, Brennan et al., 2025, *Science Advances*](https://www.science.org/doi/full/10.1126/sciadv.adr9889)).
